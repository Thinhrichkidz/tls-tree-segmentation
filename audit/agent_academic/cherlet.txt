Abstract

Terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) has revolutionized forest measurement techniques by
providing detailed three-dimensional (3D) point cloud data that captures the structure of
forests and individual trees. Instance segmentation of point clouds, i.e. separating the forest
into individual tree point clouds, remains a key challenge in automated processing due to
complex, diverse tree structure and interactions. Furthermore, comparing segmentation
performance is difficult, as new methods are often tested on new data with varying evaluation
practices. Establishing a standardized benchmark and evaluation pipeline is key to consistent
comparison and development of new algorithms and models. To this end, we manually
segmented point clouds of four different forest types into almost 3000 individual trees spanning
over 2.7 hectares. We then evaluated five open-source segmentation methods, three theory-
driven and two deep learning-based, using an evaluation pipeline with both plot and tree-scale
metrics, independent of downstream application. Our results showed that a graph-based
approach currently outperforms data-driven models for metrics such as plot-level F1-score
and tree-level mean F1 score. Segmentation performance varied greatly across forest types,
underscoring that instance segmentation remains difficult to automate and highlighting the
need for diverse training and evaluation data. The benchmark dataset and evaluation code
are publicly available to facilitate development and evaluation of generalized automated
segmentation methods.

Keywords: Terrestrial laser scanning, forest point clouds, instance segmentation,
benchmark, 3D deep learning
   1. Introduction

Laser scanning is an active remote sensing technique that is used to create highly detailed
three-dimensional representations of forests. Unmanned aerial vehicle laser scanning (UAV-
LS) provides rapid, large-scale mapping from above the canopy, while mobile laser scanning
(MLS), particularly when integrated with advancements in robotics and simultaneous
localization and mapping (SLAM) algorithms (Freißmuth et al. 2024), is used for efficient
forest data acquisition from below the canopy. At plot scales (typically up to 1 hectare),
terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) remains the standard for capturing high-resolution, ground-
based point clouds, especially in dense understory and occluded regions, due to its high
data precision and accuracy (Bienert et al. 2018). Its use in forests dates back to the early
2000s for inventory tasks, such as tree height and diameter at breast height (DBH)
estimation (Hopkinson et al., 2004; Lovell et al., 2003). In addition, TLS data may be used to
derive tree attributes that are not directly measurable using conventional inventory
techniques. Furthermore, forest aboveground biomass (AGB) estimation from TLS data
shows better accuracy and reduced bias as compared to traditional methods (Demol et al.,
2022) and enables new insights into the distribution of this biomass in forests (Calders et al.,
2015). A crucial step in the processing pipeline of TLS forest point clouds for these
applications and others is instance segmentation, i.e. separating individual trees within the
forest. This enables both explicit 3D modeling of trees by way of so-called Quantitative
Structure Models (QSMs) (Raumonen et al., 2013) and for more detailed traits (e.g. crown
dimensions) to be extracted from the point cloud directly (Terryn et al., 2023). However,
instance segmentation remains the most challenging part of automated processing pipelines
(Martin-Ducup et al., 2021). Due to overlapping crowns, dense vegetation, and varying
growth patterns, manual intervention is often needed to resegment parts of the crown
material or separate interwoven instances (Calders et al., 2015).

Automated instance segmentation has generally been performed by theory-driven algorithms
based on the identification and reconstruction of geometric features. The first widely adopted
methods identify tree trunks and use local geometry and distance-based metrics to build up
trees from there (Burt et al., 2019; Trochta et al., 2017). Further developments employ more
elaborate geometric and graph-based strategies (Wilkes et al., 2023; Xi and Hopkinson,
2022) or use ecological theory, such as metabolic scaling, to guide tree segmentation
(Wang, 2020). However, the diversity in forest and tree structure and complexity makes it
challenging to define a fixed set of features and assumptions that consistently yield strong
segmentation performance across different forest types. Even with careful hyperparameter
adjustments, theory-driven methods often cannot account for the diversity in tree structure
and interactions. Additionally, making these structural assumptions to guide segmentation is
to be avoided if the resulting data is used to estimate or test these kinds of relationships, e.g.
testing metabolic scaling theory (Yang et al., 2024). Therefore, recent work has been
focused on applying data-driven deep learning in forestry settings, which obtains state-of-the
art performance in many computer vision tasks. Deep learning models learn complex
relations directly from labeled training data, without the need for user-defined
hyperparameters, rules, or assumptions. Current models aimed at forest instance
segmentation are based on grouping, where the offset of each point is predicted and
instances are obtained by clustering (Henrich et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2023). An additional
advantage of these models is their potential for generalization to a single sensor and scale-
agnostic model (Henrich and van Delden, 2024; Wielgosz et al., 2024).
Outside forests, instance segmentation in point clouds has many applications, including
autonomous driving and robot navigation (Nguyen and Le, 2013). Method development is
driven by the comparison of segmentation results on large, standardized benchmarks, such
as ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017), Stanford 3D Indoor Scene dataset (S3DIS) (Armeni et al., 2016),
and Semantic Terrain Points Labeling - Synthetic 3D (STPLS3D) (Chen et al., 2022). Online
leaderboards display new developments over time on a set of standardized metrics, offering
a chronological overview of improvements. However, the problem of segmentation in built
indoor or urban environments with more constrained geometry and scale is very different from
that in forests, and how transferable methods are across these domains remains an open
question. Method development and advancements in forest point cloud segmentation could
benefit greatly from these benchmarking procedures and standardized evaluation. For forests,
new segmentation methods are usually developed and tested on custom datasets and with
custom evaluation practices. This individualized benchmarking complicates method
intercomparison, as segmentation difficulty may differ substantially between forest plots and
types, and performance may vary greatly depending on evaluation practices. A diverse,
standardized benchmark is needed for a fair and objective comparison of method performance
across forest types (Lines et al., 2022). Recently, such a multi-plot dataset alongside proposed
evaluation practices and training-testing split was released for UAV-LS forest data (Puliti et
al., 2023), covering eucalypt, coniferous and deciduous forests. While some labeled datasets
exist for ground-based point clouds (Calders et al. 2022; Tockner et al. 2022), no similar
machine learning ready benchmarking dataset that spans multiple forest types exists for TLS
data, and no comparison between theory-driven methods and deep-learning models has been
performed on such a dataset.

To address this gap, we created a new benchmark dataset by manually segmenting four TLS
plot point clouds ranging from 0.25 to 1.2 hectares. The plots were selected to represent a
range of forest types: tropical savanna, temperate broadleaf, temperate conifer, and tropical
rainforest. In total, 2.7 hectares were segmented, resulting in 2983 individual tree instances.
Additionally, we used a standardized evaluation pipeline to compare the performance of
instance segmentation methods on TLS data. This evaluation pipeline was applied to three
open-source theory-driven and two deep learning methods, allowing for intercomparison of
segmentation performance across forest types, independent of downstream application.

2.1 Data

The benchmark dataset introduced in this work consists of four manually segmented areas
across various forest types. Initial manual segmentation across all the plots was divided
between nine operators, all experienced with forest point cloud data collection and processing.
Segmentation was performed by randomly selecting a tree, clipping it from the environment
using a 3D selection tool, and moving it to a separate point cloud. After this initial
segmentation, a single operator checked and corrected all trees to ensure consistent
segmentation quality and level of detail throughout the plots. When segmentation is finished,
all labeled trees are merged with ground, understory, and other unlabeled points. At no point
in the segmentation are any points removed from the point cloud, to ensure a realistic testing
environment. The study areas were chosen to represent diverse forest ecosystems (Fig. 2,
Table 1). Height and DBH distributions were derived from the individual tree point clouds (Fig.
3), using the R package ITSMe (Terryn et al. 2023).

Each plot was divided into a training (60%) and validation area (20%) used by data-driven
algorithms and a testing area (20%) used for evaluation of all algorithms, by dividing the
segmented area into rectangular subplots (Fig. 4-7). Additionally, trees in the testing area were
divided into evaluation and edge trees, based on their overlap with the testing subplot. A
threshold of 90% was used to avoid exclusion of large trees with a small part of their crown
outside of this area. Points belonging to edge trees were kept in the plot to simulate a realistic
testing environment but were not used when calculating evaluation metrics. Plot stem density
[stems/ha] was calculated using the number of trunks within the annotated area, with trunk
position determined at breast height and total crown area [m2/ha] was calculated by fitting a
concave hull around the projected point cloud of trees within the annotated area, with points
clipped to this area (i.e. projected area outside of the annotated area was not counted). .

2.1.1 Litchfield

A 1-hectare plot in the tropical savanna of Litchfield National Park in Northern Australia was
scanned in August and September 2019, pre- and post-fire disturbance (Steffen, 2021). The
plot is mostly populated by Eucalyptus trees, such as Eucalyptus miniata and Eucalyptus
tetrodonta. Lidar data was collected using a RIEGL VZ-2000i with a pulse repetition rate of
600 kHz and angular resolution of 0.03° on a regularly spaced 20 by 20 meter grid. The
registered point cloud was downsampled to a resolution of 1cm. All trees taller than 5 meters
were extracted manually from the pre-fire data using CloudCompare, corresponding to a
minimal DBH of 3.5 cm. In total, 703 trees were labeled, of which 128 were used for evaluation
(Fig. 4).

2.1.2 Wytham Woods

A 6-hectare area was scanned in Wytham Woods, Oxford, UK, from late November 2015 to
January 2016 (Calders et al., 2022a). The forest is mostly populated by deciduous trees, such
as Sycamore maple [Acer pseudoplatanus], European ash [Fraxinus excelsior], and Common
hazel [Corylus avellana]. Data was collected using a RIEGL VZ-400 scanner with a pulse
repetition rate of 300 kHz and angular resolution of 0.04°. A 20 by 20 meter grid was used,
with an upright and tilted scan at each location. The data was further downsampled to 2.6 cm
resolution, determined based on a nearest neighbor analysis and scanner properties.
Individual trees were segmented in a 1.4 ha study area in the center of the plot, using the
treeseg algorithm (Burt et al., 2019), followed by visual inspection and manual correction
(Calders et al., 2018). All trees were segmented from the plot, resulting in a minimum tree
height and DBH of 2.23 m and 2.4 cm respectively, as derived from the point clouds. Tree
labels were propagated back to the plot points to obtain a fully annotated plot point cloud.
Extensive quality control was performed to obtain consistent segmentation quality with the
other plots. Finally, the study area was downsized to eliminate unannotated edge points,
resulting in a final annotated area of 135 by 88 meters, or 1.2 ha. 876 trees were labeled, of
which 181 were used for evaluation (Fig. 5).

2.1.3 Ofental

A 1-hectare plot in Ofental, Germany, was scanned from June to July 2023. Forest census
data from 2022 shows that the plot is mostly dominated by two coniferous species: Norway
spruce [Picea abies] (93.8 %) and European larch [Larix decidua] (4.2 %), besides some
individual broadleaved trees (mostly Sycamore maple [Acer pseudoplatanus] and beech
[Fagus sylvatica]). The plot is located near the Bavarian Alps and features alpine terrain. The
average slope of the terrain in the segmented area, as calculated from the point cloud, is 32
degrees. Forest structure is mostly shaped by past land use, which resulted in even-aged
trees with dense structure. A RIEGL VZ-400i scanner, using a pulse repetition rate of 600 kHz
and angular resolution of 0.04°, was positioned on a regular 10 by 10 meter rectangular grid,
with upright and tilted scans at each position for a total of 240 scans. The registered point
cloud was downsampled to a resolution of 1 cm. A 50 by 50 meter area around the center of
the plot was segmented manually. All trees were extracted from the plot, resulting in a minimal
height and DBH in the dataset of 2.3 m and 4 cm. A total of 464 trees were labeled, of which
89 were used for evaluation (Fig. 6).

2.2 Evaluation

In this section, the evaluation methodology and metrics for segmentation performance are
described. A diagram of the evaluation workflow can be found in Fig. 8. Additionally, method
execution was timed and compared. All method runs were executed on a Linux desktop with
an Intel Xeon 36 3.00 GHz core CPU with 192 Gb RAM and an Nvidia A-4000 GPU with 48
Gb VRAM.
                         Fig. 8. Diagram of the evaluation pipeline and metrics.

The first step in evaluating model performance is to establish optimal assignments between
the set of manually segmented instances and the set of predictions, i.e., deciding which tree
to compare to which prediction. As there is no specific order to these sets, this must be
performed based on the individual point clouds of the evaluation trees and the predictions.
While matching using application-specific geometric features, such as extracted trunk location,
has previously been applied in forest segmentation work (Wilkes et al., 2023), we opted for a
more general bipartite graph matching, commonly used in computer vision literature (Henrich
et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019). A cost matrix between the manually segmented and prediction
sets was constructed by calculating the Intersection over Union (IoU) of each predicted tree 
with each manually segmented tree t (Eq. 1). As there is no explicit order to the points within
each cloud and to avoid floating-point precision issues, IoU calculation is performed using a
distance-based point comparison.

A tree is successfully detected if the IoU with its matched prediction is higher than or equal to
0.5, ensuring a unique assignment (Kirillov et al., 2019). This thresholding allows us to derive
simple plot-level metrics. The first is plot-level recall (eq. 2), or the fraction of evaluation trees

successfully matched predictions. These are calculated using True Positive (TP), False
Positive (FP), and False Negative (FN) instances. At the plot scale, the TP set consists of tree
instances with successful predictions, the FP set consists of predictions without matching
evaluation instances, and the FN set consists of evaluation trees without matching predictions.
These metrics indicate the performance of a method for detecting tree instances over a plot
but do not indicate how well successful predictions are segmented in detail.

At the individual tree scale, we evaluated how well successfully matched trees were

with tree-level TP, FP, and FN sets. In contrast to the plot-level definitions, here TP, FP, and

Precision and recall can then be calculated for each successful match based on these point

sets. Furthermore, the F1 score is the harmonic mean of these measures and serves as a

useful metric to assess overall performance. These were then averaged over all successful

and occlusion, leaves and smaller branches at the top, and understory, terrain, and buttresses

at the bottom. Further from the tree center, segmentation performance will likely decrease due

3. Algorithms

Only open-source methods that work on entire TLS plot point clouds were considered for this
work. This benchmark is not meant as an exhaustive review of instance segmentation
methods, but serves as a starting point for the intercomparison of different segmentation
strategies and a step towards standardization of method comparison. The methods for
instance segmentation can be broadly divided into two categories: theory-driven and deep
learning-based methods.

3.1 Theory-driven methods

Theory-driven methods segment tree instances based on domain-specific, handcrafted
rulesets and geometric features. Usually, tree instances are detected by identifying tree trunks
and building up the tree from there, using clustering or graph-based algorithms. These
methods typically depend on a set of hyperparameters, which enable the user to adjust the
algorithm to a particular dataset. For this benchmarking exercise, only limited hyperparameter
adjustment was executed, details for each method are described in the sections below. An
experienced operator with intricate knowledge of how the hyperparameters of different steps
interact may be able to obtain better results.

3.1.1 Rayextract

RayCloudTools (Lowe and Stepanas, 2021) is a set of command line tools with an associated
C++ library designed for efficient processing and analysis of point clouds and associated ray
information. Rayextract is one of these commands, which provides functionality for extracting
both terrain and trees from a forest ray cloud. Point clouds can easily be converted to ray
clouds using the rayimport command. The tree segmentation algorithm behind the Rayextract
command uses Dijkstra's algorithm to find the shortest paths between the vertices of the
terrain mesh as root points and the points of the ray cloud. Edge weights used as scoring
metrics for Dijkstra's algorithm are based on the distance between neighboring paths, where
additionally paths with consistent direction or belonging to trees with a large estimated height
are favored, whereas paths with strong lateral movement are penalized. Afterwards, paths are
agglomerated into separate trees. Rayextract exposes a number of hyperparameters to
customize performance. For the terrain extraction step, the default value of the gradient
hyperparameter (1) gave adequate results. The height_min hyperparameter was set based on
the minimum height of the trees in the training and validation set, and can be customized
based on the user's application. The max_diameter hyperparameter was not extracted from
the segmented trees but estimated from the raw plot point cloud, by visualizing a slice of the
point cloud, identifying large trunks and estimating the maximum DBH using a measuring tool.
The values can be found in Table A.1 in the appendix. Default values were used for the other
hyperparameters gravity_factor (0.3) and distance_limit (1.0).

3.1.2 Treeiso

Treeiso (Xi and Hopkinson, 2022) is a three-step graph-based method. In the first step, the
cut-pursuit algorithm is used to segment the point cloud, represented as a graph with points
as nodes and the inverse distance of neighboring points as edge weights, into small clusters
that represent coherent patches of branches and crowns. In the second step, this algorithm is
repeated on a graph of 2D (XY) cluster centroids, with the minimum Euclidean distance in 3D
space between clusters as edge weights. The final step attempts to combat oversegmentation
of trees with significant horizontal crown spacing. Segments containing tree stems are
detected by considering the ratio of elevation to length. The remaining segments are then
assigned to the nearest stem segment. All three steps of the algorithm have several
hyperparameters to be adjusted (Table A.2 in the Appendix).

3.1.3 SSSC

SSSC (Wang, 2020) is an unsupervised pipeline for joint instance and semantic segmentation.
In the first shared step, an undirected superpoint graph is constructed by recursive
segmentation of the point cloud into small, geometrically similar parts. Embeddings for each
superpoint are described by a number of point cloud features, as well as an initial wood
probability estimated based on local linearity. Edges in the superpoint graph are constructed
based on a k-nearest neighbor graph of the original point cloud. The initial wood probability is
refined by considering neighboring superpoints and used to identify tree roots. All superpoints
are then assigned to root superpoints using Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm, and tree labels
are propagated to points within superpoints accordingly. As SSSC only displays one
hyperparameter, ft_threshold, without documentation, no adjustments were made and the
default value of 0.125 was used for all plots.

3.1.4 Terrain removal and normalization

Both Treeiso and SSSC require removal of ground points before segmentation. To this end,
we used the Simple Morphological (SMRF) filter implemented in PDAL (Butler et al., 2024),
which performed well in a forest and steppe ground removal survey (Moudrý et al., 2020).
Different hyperparameter combinations were tested, but visually had no significant effect on
ground removal quality. Additionally, SSSC requires height normalization. For this, we used
the PDAL Height Above Ground Nearest Neighbor filter. As point order is not maintained by
the SSSC algorithm, predictions with the original point height were obtained by distance-based
propagation of labels to the unedited, normalized point cloud and subsequent reversal of the
normalization.

3.2 Deep learning methods

In contrast to theory-driven methods, deep learning methods do not use expert-designed
features to infer tree instances, but learn these features directly from labeled data. These
models depend on a large backbone for feature extraction, followed by a
segmentation/clustering step based on per-voxel features. While early research focused on
point-based feature networks for computational feasibility, advancements in sparse tensor
network development have enabled the use of large 3D sparse convolutional U-networks. Both
methods listed below use such a U-network as their feature extraction backbone, followed by
a semantic head for tree classification and a clustering step to identify instances.

3.2.1 TreeLearn

The TreeLearn (Henrich et al. 2024) model consists of a sparse convolutional U-net, followed
by a semantic and offset prediction head. The semantic segmentation head consists of a two-
layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with shared weights, trained to predict whether a point
belongs to a tree. Similarly, the offset prediction head consists of a three-layer MLP, trained
to predict the vector of tree points to the tree base, defined as the center of the trunk at three
meters high. If the offset and semantic networks were perfect, the output of this step would be
sets of points representing individual trees at their base. In reality, clustering is performed to
assign points to tree instances. This is done in two steps. First, trunk points are identified
based on a local verticality and base offset metric calculated on the original input point cloud.
These trunk points are then clustered based on the offset-projected coordinates in the XY
plane. Secondly, the remaining tree points are assigned to tree clusters using k-nearest
neighbors in the projected coordinate space.
In this work, we performed multiple finetuning runs and compared to the original, pretrained
models, for which weights are available online (Henrich, van Delden, Kneib, et al. 2023).
These weights were obtained by pretraining on a set of 19 automatically labeled plots
dominated by European beech (Fagus sylvatica L.), followed by finetuning on three public,
manually labelled datasets: FOR-Instance (Puliti et al. 2023), LAUTx (Tockner et al. 2022) and
Wytham Woods. As our test data is present in the training data of these weights, for the
evaluation of the original weights on Wytham Woods and as a starting point for our finetuning
runs, a different set of weights were used, also available online. The training procedure for
these weights is described in the TreeLearn preprint (v2) (Henrich, van Delden, Seidel, et al.
2023), consisting of the same 19 automatically labelled plots, and finetuning on a single
manually labelled plot.
For each plot, a separate finetuning run is done on the training and validation areas of that
plot. Additionally, we finetuned a single model on the training and validation data of all four
plots. We further differentiate between these models using finetuned (single plot) and
finetuned (all plots). All finetuning experiments were done over 250 epochs, using the AdamW
optimizer with batch size 2, initial learning rate 0.0015 and cosine learning rate scheduler. The
checkpoint with minimal offset loss on the validation set was used for inference.

3.2.2 ForAINet

ForAINet (Xiang et al. 2024) is a deep learning framework for automated segmentation and
retrieval of forest stand and tree parameters. The instance segmentation backbone was first
introduced by Xiang et al. in 2023, where different bottom-up approaches to large-scale
panoptic (instance + semantic) segmentation were investigated. The same deep learning
backbone was used in the subsequent SegmentAnyTree model (Wielgosz et al. 2024).
Similar to TreeLearn, a 3D neural network is used to obtain per-point tree features that serve
as inputs to three segmentation branches. The first branch performs semantic segmentation
using a small neural network with a single hidden layer. The center offset branch employs
the same strategy as TreeLearn, aiming to predict the offset vector to its instance center for
each point. A final instance embedding branch embeds each point in a 5D feature space
optimized to separate instances spatially. Clustering is then applied to both branches, and
results are combined using a final small scoring network. Their experiments showed that the
combination of these clustering strategies increased instance segmentation performance
compared to using only one strategy.
Analogous to TreeLearn, we performed finetuning on the plots individually, as well as all four
plots simultaneously. We further refer to the resulting models as finetuned (single plot) and
finetuned (all plots) respectively. All runs use the hyperparameter settings and TreeMix
augmentation which resulted in the highest F1-score on the FOR-Instance dataset.
Finetuning was done over 40 epochs, using the Adam optimizer with batch size 4, initial
learning rate 0.0005 and exponential learning rate scheduler. The checkpoint with minimal
F1-score on the validation data was used for inference.

4. Results

Segmentation performance was evaluated at two scales. At the plot scale, recall indicates
the fraction of manually segmented trees that were successfully detected. Precision
indicates the fraction of predictions that were successful. F1 is the harmonic mean of these
two metrics, and indicates overall plot-level performance. At the tree scale, mRecall and
mPrecision indicate the point-level performance of each method, averaged over successful
predictions. mF1 is the harmonic mean of these two metrics. These tree-level metrics were
further binned by relative tree height and relative distance from the center to obtain a more
fine-grained view of tree-level performance.
In subsection 4.1, the performance of the original weights provided by the authors of
TreeLearn and ForAINet are compared to that of the finetuned models, both those trained on
each single plot and those trained on all four plots at once. In subsection 4.2, we compare
the performance of the models finetuned on all four plots to that of the theory-driven
methods. Finally, running times for all methods are compared in subsection 4.3.

5. Discussion

5.1 Plot-level comparison

Segmentation performance varied greatly across different forest types. In Litchfield, the most
sparse plot in the benchmark with the lowest total projected crown area, Rayextract,
TreeLearn and ForAINet obtained plot-level metrics and tree-level mF1 of around or over 0.9,
as compared to manual segmentation. Rayextract achieved a precision of over 0.95, while
Treeiso and SSSC had a higher number of false predictions, likely caused by remnants of
terrain, shrubs and young, small trees. However, plot-level recall is arguably more important
to evaluate forest segmentation than plot-level precision, as it is more difficult and time-
consuming to segment additional missing instances than filtering out additional false positives
(manually or automatically, e.g., filtering instances on a minimal height). There is a higher
variability in method performance on the Wytham Woods plot. It has the lowest stem density
of the four plots but a relatively large total projected crown area, indicating large, horizontally
spread trees, which may cause lower performance as compared to Litchfield. While Ofental is
a more dense plot than Wytham Woods regarding the number of stems, the total crown area
is similar. This is likely due to the high number of conifers with a vertical growing patterns (Fig.
6). Additionally, the sloped, alpine terrain may complicate segmentation in the lower regions
of the trees. Performance on Ofental was quite similar to Wytham Woods across different
methods. However, tree-level performance binned by relative tree height did not show a
decrease in performance close to the ground, while performance binned by relative distance
from the center decreased strongly away from the tree center (Fig. 10), which suggests that
not the terrain but the interactions the dense neighborhood of trees limit performance. The plot
stem density combined with the structural homogeneity of the trees likely complicated
segmentation in the outer branches (Fig. 11). Finally, Robson Creek is densely populated by
varying tree species and features a dense understory, lianas, and epiphytes. It has the highest
stem density and total crown area of the four plots. All methods performed the worst on this
complex plot by a significant margin, with the best-performing Rayextract detecting just over
half of the trees at a precision of 0.46. The variation in method performance across forest
types emphasizes the need for diverse datasets to develop and test generalized methods.

5.2 Theory-driven methods

Rayextract was among the best methods across all plots and metrics. Furthermore, the
RayCloudTools toolset, of which the method is a part, provides its own tool for terrain
extraction and requires no height normalization (Lowe and Stepanas, 2021). Combining a
distance-based graph algorithm with forest-specific knowledge worked well, especially in
structurally complex plots where the other clustering-based theory-driven methods fell short.

Treeiso and SSSC obtained lower performance on this benchmark than on their custom
datasets. Xi and Hopkinson (2022) report an average plot-level recall of 0.86 and an average
average IoU of detected trees (closely related to mF1) of 0.92 for the Treeiso algorithm on a
dataset of 16 plots mostly populated by coniferous or deciduous species. Wang (2020) reports
that SSSC obtains an average plot-level recall of 0.762 and a precision of 0.763, on a dataset
of 6 plots dominated by coniferous species and birch trees. The discrepancy in performance
on our benchmark and the custom datasets is likely due to the requirement of terrain and
understory removal inherent to Treeiso and SSSC. Automated ground removal algorithms are
often designed for low-density aerial laser scanning point clouds and struggle with the level of
detail and presence of understory points in point clouds acquired with TLS. In their work,
Treeiso authors manually removed the ground and understory for evaluation. The authors of
SSSC, on the other hand, mention that ground removal is out of the scope of their work (Wang,
2020). However, we argue that differentiating between ground, understory vegetation, and
tree points is one of the key challenges of instance segmentation in forest settings. In other
theory-driven methods, this is solved by integrating a dedicated, deep-learning semantic
segmentation step, separating ground, wood and leaf points (Wielgosz et al., 2023; Wilkes et
al., 2023). The assumption of perfect ground and understory removal likely significantly
reduced the performance of these methods in this benchmark.

5.3 Deep learning methods

On the deep learning side, we observed the TreeLearn model generally outperforming the
ForAINet model, especially at the tree level. One explanation could be that the TreeLearn
model is pretrained on an automatically labeled dataset. Even though all these plots are
populated by European beech, a species not present in our dataset, this pretraining allows the
model to learn general tree features from a larger set of labeled trees. This provides the model
with rough initial weights that allow for more efficient finetuning on different forest types.
Another possible explanation is the two-stage, forest-specific clustering used in the TreeLearn
model. First, clusters are identified from points with a high likelihood of belonging to a tree
trunk, and the remaining points are assigned to these candidate instances. In this way, the
TreeLearn method incorporates some forest domain knowledge to improve the results of the
data-driven model. An exception is the Robson Creek plot, where ForAINet detected
significantly more trees than the TreeLearn model with similar tree-level performance. The 5D
embedding branch, where 3D points are projected into a higher dimensional feature space
offering more representation capacity, may thus be useful for discerning instances in dense,
complex plots. On the other hand, the forest-specific assumptions employed in the TreeLearn
clustering, which was developed and tested using mostly data from deciduous forests, may
not hold up in this tropical forest. A combination of both models, employing the 5D embedding
branch with a forest-specific clustering strategy, may improve results in some cases. Finally,
a slight decrease in performance at the base of the trees was observed for all algorithms, i.e.
where the tree was segmented from the terrain. The greater decrease for Robson Creek is
likely due to the presence of buttresses, which can span across several meters horizontally.
This effect is noticeably less pronounced for TreeLearn, showcasing the advantage of data-
driven methods to learn more irregular patterns that may be hard to integrate into a theory-
driven algorithm.

Both models used in this benchmark have a semantic branch, allowing for classification of
points into a semantic class. This allows the model to separate terrain, low vegetation and
noise points and perform instance segmentation only on the tree points, without any pre-
processing. As we only present a binary classification (non-tree and tree) in this work, we did
not explicitly evaluate the semantic segmentation performance of these models. However, if
training data with more extensive labels, such as the stem, woody branches, live branches,
terrain and low vegetation classes presented in the FOR-instance UAV-LS dataset, these
models have the potential for panoptic segmentation, the process of simultaneous semantic
and instance segmentation.
The advantage of deep learning models is their ability to learn patterns directly from data
without the need for human-designed feature extraction and heuristics. As is standard practice
in benchmarking exercises, the deep learning architectures presented have been fine-tuned
and tested on in-domain data, in this case a training, validation and testing crop from the same
plot. We show that finetuning these models significantly improves segmentation results,
highlighting the need for a large number of diverse training data. However, an end-user usually
does not have segmented data available to fine-tune deep learning models. As the testing
data originates from the same plot as the training and validation data, these models might be
slightly overfit to the exact characteristics of the specific forest. For example, the performance
of the models on the Robson Creek area might not be entirely representative of these models
on other tropical forest plots. An interesting future research direction would be to experiment
with how much segmented data is needed to significantly improve performance on a specific
plot. Furthermore, while adjusting the hyperparameters of theory-driven methods often
depends on expert knowledge and can be a time-consuming, laborious process, these
adjustments enable users to customize or adapt the method to different sensor and plot types
and potentially improve segmentation performance on diverse data. On the other hand, after
training, deep learning models are mostly black-box and offer limited customization. Recent
work demonstrated the potential for developing generalized models capable of segmenting
data from a range of sensors and forest types. (Henrich and van Delden, 2024; Wielgosz et
al., 2024). However, more work and data is needed to test the adaptation and finetuning of
these models to more diverse and complicated forest types.

5.4 Benchmark considerations

While the four plots were scanned with similar protocols and instruments, slight differences in
the data may complicate performance comparison across plots. First, three different
instruments were used in data collection (RIEGL VZ-2000i, RIEGL VZ-400, and RIEGL VZ-
400i). However, scanner characteristics between these instruments are very similar and the
data is processed using the same software and algorithms. Additionally, Litchfield and Wytham
Woods are scanned on a 20 by 20 meter grid, while Ofental and Robson Creek are scanned
on a 10 by 10 meter grid, resulting in reduced occlusion and a more uniform sampling pattern
(Wilkes et al., 2017). The downsampling of each plot reduces the variance in point cloud
density but does not eliminate the increased occlusion introduced by the more sparse
sampling pattern. Furthermore, Wytham Woods was downsampled to 2.6 cm based on a
nearest-neighbor analysis outside the context of this work (Calders et al., 2018), while all three
of the other plots were downsampled to 1 cm. Despite these differences, all data acquisitions
meet the minimum requirements for geometric modelling of AGB reference data of the CEOS
WGCV Land Product Validation sub-group (Duncanson et al., 2021, pp. 63-65). Furthermore,
the theory-driven methods are designed to be robust for varying inter-point distances, by e.g.,
allowing for gaps in the point cloud using a max-gap hyperparameter of 2 meter for Treeiso
and distance-limit hyperparameter of 1 meter for Rayextract, or using a distance-independent
neighborhood size for clustering. Additionally, the deep-learning models voxelize the point
clouds to a resolution of 10 cm prior to execution. As such, we believe this discrepancy does
not have a large effect on plot intercomparison of segmentation performance. Finally, some
differences between the segmentation protocols for different plots may introduce some bias in
the comparison between plots. For both Litchfield and Robson Creek, a height threshold of 5
meters was used. For both Wytham Woods and Ofental, all trees were segmented, until no
more tree candidates could be identified in the plot point cloud, resulting in a minimal height
of 2.23 m and 2.3 m respectively. However, we note that the finetuned deep learning models
obtain well over 90% semantic accuracy (tree vs. non-tree) on all plots, which shows that the
limiting factor in their instance segmentation performance is not the classification of trees from
the ground and understory points, but the delineation of trees from each other. Furthermore,
Rayextract offers the min_height hyperparameter to customize the model to each plot.
Therefore, we believe these differences have a limited effect on the benchmark results.

The rectangular plot shape introduces floating vegetation and cut-off branches at the edge of
the plot. Due to these artifacts, segmentation performance likely decreases near the edge for
all methods. In practice, these edge effects can be avoided by applying the algorithms on a
region of interest (ROI) with a certain buffer and keeping only the predicted trees within the
ROI, given that the plot point cloud is of sufficient quality outside this ROI. In our study, due to
the partitioning of each plot into testing, validation, and training areas, and the already limited
plot size, this type of buffering was not possible. These effects may be stronger for explicit 3D
clustering-based methods such as Treeiso, TreeLearn, and ForAINet, as all tree points that
are classified as belonging to a tree will be assigned to an instance. On the other hand, graph-
based algorithms, such as Rayextract and SCCC, that explicitly build up individual trees from
the ground up, may be able to handle these artifacts better. However, edge effects are present
in both theory-driven and deep learning methods, and the overall performance on this
benchmark is thus skewed lower for all methods than what would be possible on buffered
data.

Finally, manual segmentation is still regarded as the most accurate segmentation method, yet
data quality may decrease due to the labor- and time-intensiveness of this task (Wilkes et al.,
2023). We attempted to minimize these mistakes and inter-operator subjectivity by performing
manual quality control in a second segmentation iteration. However, operator bias is still likely
to occur in areas with complex tree-environment interactions, which is impossible to derive
from quantitative evaluation metrics.

Despite these limitations, the manually segmented dataset that is part of this study is a step
forward in the direction toward a standardized development and evaluation workflow for TLS
instance segmentation. To our knowledge, this is the largest and most diverse dataset of
segmented TLS plots and trees (Henrich and van Delden, 2024), spanning over 2.7 hectares
and almost 3000 trees. Additionally, fine-tuning data-driven methods is supported by the test,
validation, and training split.

5.5 Future work

Standardized, quantitative metrics are essential for comparison and tracking development of
segmentation methods. However, methods that achieve similar scores may display
significantly different patterns in their errors, and lead to different results and performance
dependent on the user's application. Therefore, qualitative assessment of segmentation
quality likely will remain a key component of semi-automatic processing pipelines.
Furthermore, common downstream applications, like automatic forest inventory and biomass
estimation through tree modelling, require application-specific evaluation practices to study
the effects of segmentation methods. Quantitative analysis of the effects of different instance
segmentation methods as compared to manual segmentation is a promising research
direction and a key factor of future method development and comparison.

Additionally, the performance of these deep learning methods is likely limited by the amount
of training data available. The size of the training data presented in this work remains small
compared to indoor and urban datasets such as S3DIS (Armeni et al., 2016) and STPLS3D
(Chen et al., 2022). As manual segmentation, especially in high-resolution TLS data from
complex, dense forests like Ofental and Robson Creek, is incredibly time-consuming,
exploiting unlabeled data through self-supervised pretraining may be a useful direction for
increasing segmentation performance in these deep learning models (Zeng et al., 2024).

Finally, this work does not include an extensive review and evaluation of all available
segmentation methods. Therefore, an essential next step is establishing an online benchmark,
with data from multiple sources, where developers of new models and methods can upload
and compare results in real-time.
6. Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a standardized TLS benchmark consisting of four manually
segmented forest plots of high data quality, along with a robust evaluation pipeline. The forest
plots were chosen to represent varying forest complexity and ecosystems. The dataset, along
with application-independent evaluation, allowed fair benchmarking of instance segmentation
methods using five open-source algorithms for TLS forest point clouds. Performance greatly
varied across the four forest plots, highlighting the need for diverse training and testing data
for the development of generalized segmentation algorithms. Results showed that the graph-
based Rayextract algorithm currently generally outperforms data-driven methods, which may
be limited by the available training data. Comparison of two deep learning models revealed
that integrating forest-specific knowledge into model design could improve segmentation
results. Combining forest-specific algorithms with data-driven feature extraction may be the
key to automating instance segmentation across forest types. The dataset and benchmark
results of this study can be used to advance future method development and comparison.
