BOX 1 Glossary of key terms

Absolute geolocation accuracy: The quality of geolocation information with respect to a well-defined coordinate
reference system.

    Angular resolution: The smallest separation between two surfaces, measured as an angle, that can be distin-
guished by a lidar sensor. In general, higher quality lidar sensors have finer angular resolution (i.e., can distin-
guish finer features) than lower quality lidar sensors. Angular resolution may differ in the vertical and
horizontal dimensions.

    Beam divergence: All laser beams have a width, sometimes called the laser footprint. Beam divergence is the
angle at which the laser beam width increases with distance. Beam divergence is a characteristic important for
understanding the limitations of different sensors because lidar measurements close to the instrument reflect
light from a smaller area than measurements far from the sensor (e.g., small beam divergence is needed to char-
acterize fine features).

    Canopy height model (CHM): A raster (gridded) product that describes the maximum height of trees above
the ground. CHMs are created by subtracting the ground elevation from a digital terrain model from the abso-
lute surface height in a digital surface model.

    Digital surface model (DSM): A raster (gridded) product that describes the absolute maximum height of the
canopy surface in an ecosystem, typically in units of elevation in a known coordinate reference system. The
DSM height reflects both the height of trees and the elevation of the ground.

    Digital terrain model (DTM): A raster (gridded) product that describes the ground elevation, typically in a
known coordinate reference system.

    Footprint: The laser footprint refers to the cross-sectional area of a laser beam, which depends on both beam
divergence and the distance from the lidar instrument.

    Instance segmentation: Instance segmentation is the process of identifying and delineating individual objects
(e.g., trees) in a dataset (e.g., a lidar point cloud).

    Intensity: The intensity of a lidar measurement is an estimate of the magnitude of reflected energy detected
by the sensor. Intensity is determined by the reflectance of the reflecting surface in the wavelength of the lidar
laser, and by observational properties like the distance from the sensor and the incidence angle. Some
post-processing software accounts for those observational properties to estimate reflectance, in addition to
intensity.

    Leaf area index/density: The total one-sided amount of leaf area per unit ground area (leaf area index, LAI)
or per unit volume (leaf area density, LAD).

    Lidar: Light detection and ranging (lidar) is a remote sensing technology that uses lasers to characterize
three-dimensional (3D) structure of objects (e.g., vegetation and ground) through properties of reflected light.

    Mesh: Meshes use flexible geometries such as triangles to represent surfaces detected with lidar data.
Meshes can enable volume estimation, visualization, and data size reduction compared to point clouds.

    Occlusion: Occlusion is when objects (e.g., wood, leaves) obstruct the path of lasers such that some areas
within a scene are not sampled in lidar data. Canopy occlusion is a concern for ground-based lidar platforms
(terrestrial laser scanning, TLS; mobile laser scanning, MLS), and understory or ground occlusion for air- or
spaceborne platforms (airborne laser scanning, ALS; unoccupied aerial system laser scanning, ULS; spaceborne
laser scanning, SLS).

    Plant area index/density: The total one-sided amount of plant area per unit ground area (plant area index,
PAI) or per unit volume (plant area density, PAD). Unlike leaf area index (LAI)/leaf area density (LAD),
PAI/PAD do not distinguish between leaf material and non-photosynthetic material.

    Point cloud: Point clouds represent lidar data as a collection of discrete, three-dimensional (3D) coordinates
in space where surfaces reflect laser energy. In some sensors, a single laser pulse can produce multiple points,
called multiple returns. Each point in space integrates returned energy from within the laser footprint; where
the footprint is large compared to reflecting objects, points' positions may not fall along the object surface in
space. Some sensors collect data as waveforms but process data to point clouds to reduce data size and
dimensionality.

    Quantitative structure model: Quantitative structure models represent tree woody structure and volume
using a series of geometric features (e.g., cylinders) fit to lidar point cloud data.

    Relative geolocation accuracy: The internal consistency of geolocation information within a dataset.
    Relative height metrics: A simplified representation of waveform lidar data that describes the height below
which a given percentile of returned lidar data originates. For example, the 50th relative height metrics (often
called RH50) is the median height of returned energy above the ground.
    Scan angle: The scan angle of a lidar measurement is the angle between the emitted laser pulse and nadir
(the vertical direction perpendicular to the ground).
    Semantic segmentation: Semantic segmentation is the process of assigning object classification labels
(e.g., wood, leaves, ground) to units of data (e.g., lidar points).
    Time of flight: Time of flight refers to the time between laser emission and detection of reflected laser energy
by the lidar sensor. Time of flight lidars use this time, and the known speed of light, to infer the distance to
reflecting objects. Distance, with the known sensor orientation in space, is then used to characterize
three-dimensional (3D) positions in space.
    Voxel: Voxels are rectangular prisms analogous to image pixels--where pixels are two-dimensional
(2D) units on a plane, voxels are 3D units in space. Some analyses of forest structure estimate a property
(e.g., leaf area) for all voxels in a scene.
    Waveform: Waveforms represent lidar data as the distribution of reflected energy detected by the sensor as a
function of time, where time is related to distance from the sensor. Waveforms are often depicted as continuous
curves--in practice, lidar sensors sample the detected reflected energy at very fine time intervals to construct
the waveform.

BOX 2 Lidar sampling from space

Spaceborne laser scanning (SLS) data are different from other lidar types presented here in important ways.
While other platforms collect wall-to-wall data that completely cover the sampled area (besides occlusion),
existing SLS instruments are sampling platforms, observing a subset of the Earth's surface. For example, Global
Ecosystems Dynamics Investigation (GEDI) collects measurements along eight tracks, with 60 m separating
waveforms along the same track, and 600 m between adjacent tracks. The spatial distribution of GEDI tracks is
also influenced by the altitude and path of the International Space Station (ISS). Because SLS instruments are
above the atmosphere and clouds absorb light in the wavelengths of SLS lasers, clouds reduce data quality and
availability in some areas (Potapov et al., 2021). Geolocation accuracy for SLS footprints can also complicate
comparison to other high-resolution datasets or analyses of change over time (Roy et al., 2021)­for example,
the Version 1 geolocation accuracy for GEDI footprints is 10 m (Luthcke et al., 2019), but is expected to
improve in future versions. SLS is undoubtedly a powerful tool to quantify forest structure over broad scales,
however, due to these sampling and quality considerations, there is a limit to the spatial and temporal scales
that can be reliably resolved with SLS data. Robust characterization of these limits is an area of ongoing
research.

    To illustrate this issue, we present data from the initial approximately 4-year deployment of GEDI on the
ISS. During that time, GEDI collected 33,239 samples over the 109 km2 Smithsonian Environmental Research
Center (SERC) NEON site (Figure 5). Of those, 41% (13,670) samples were classified as high quality according
to the GEDI level 2A quality flag, and 13% (4247) were high quality and collected during the growing season,
for which biomass and leaf area models were developed. In 1 ha of the SERC plot containing the transect
shown in Figure 2, GEDI collected six waveforms, including two high quality waveforms (Figure 6,
Appendix S1: Figure S2). This example demonstrates the strength of GEDI for characterizing structure and
structural variability at the landscape level, and potential limitations for characterizing structure and structural
change over individual small plots. The next generation of SLS platforms will likely have even higher potential
to cover the Earth's surface at high spatial and temporal resolution. For example, a proposed SLS mission called
the Earth Dynamics Geodetic Explorer (EDGE) would collect nearly wall-to-wall data (NewsGram 2024).
